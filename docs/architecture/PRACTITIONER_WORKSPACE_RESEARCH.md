# Practitioner and Organization Workspace Research

**Reviewed:** 2026-10-12
**Scope:** U.S.-first product and workflow review; design input, not legal advice or a claim of regulatory compliance.

## Finding

WellnessCafe currently has a single individual-provider workspace. A user gets one `provider` capability and a `providerType`; the practitioner dashboard loads the same client, appointment, availability, and shared-support panels for every provider. Applications store an organization name as profile text, but there is no organization tenant, membership, staff invite, location, or team-role model. The current service taxonomy does not include primary care, case management, community health work, nursing, pharmacy, or community supervision. Existing practitioner/client connections and client-selected information sharing are useful building blocks, but an organization relationship must not itself grant access to a person's information.

Relevant implementation points: `functions/src/practitionerRegistry.js`, `functions/src/adminUserAccess.js`, `src/apps/provider/PractitionerPortalPage.jsx`, `src/apps/provider/ProviderDashboardPage.jsx`, `src/services/practitionerRegistry.js`, and `src/apps/admin/AdminWorkspaceAccessPage.jsx`.

## What current guidance says

- **Primary care is team-based.** AHRQ's PCMH description includes physicians, advanced practice nurses, physician assistants, nurses, pharmacists, nutritionists, social workers, educators, and care coordinators; small practices may link with community services instead of employing every role. CMS's Psychiatric Collaborative Care Model describes a treating practitioner, behavioral-health care manager, and psychiatric consultant working together, supported by a patient registry and measurement-based follow-up. This argues for role-specific work queues and collaboration surfaces, not one universal clinician dashboard. [AHRQ: Defining the PCMH](https://www.ahrq.gov/ncepcr/research/care-coordination/pcmh/define.html) · [CMS: Behavioral Health Integration Services](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/mln909432-behavorial-health-integration-services.pdf)
- **Case management is coordination and client partnership.** NASW's case-management standards emphasize client self-determination, individualized goals, referrals, service-plan monitoring, informed consent, and documentation. The workflow is distinct from a medical visit or a community-supervision record. [NASW: Standards for Social Work Case Management](https://www.socialworkers.org/Practice/NASW-Practice-Standards-Guidelines/NASW-Standards-for-Social-Work-Case-Management)
- **Peer support is its own workforce.** SAMHSA describes peer workers as people with lived/living experience and identifies support, advocacy, resource linkage, community building, mentoring, goal setting, and warm handoffs as common activities. Certification requirements vary by jurisdiction and pathway; the federal model standards guide state and territorial programs rather than replacing them. Peer dashboards should support client-led contact and referrals without implying diagnosis, clinical authority, or blanket access to clinical records. [SAMHSA: Peer Worker Core Competencies](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/recovery/peer-support-workers/core-competencies) · [SAMHSA: National Model Standards for Peer Support Certification](https://library.samhsa.gov/sites/default/files/pep23-10-01-001.pdf)
- **Community supervision is not a clinical-care role.** U.S. Courts describe probation and pretrial services work as assessment, supervision plans, monitoring conditions, court reporting, and referrals to treatment, medical care, employment, and community services. These duties involve legal authority and records that are not interchangeable with a healthcare relationship. Any future probation/parole workspace needs a separate service boundary, organization verification, and jurisdiction-specific policy review; a supervision relationship must never reveal check-ins, therapy notes, practice history, AI conversations, or other clinical content by default. [U.S. Courts: Probation and Pretrial Services](https://www.uscourts.gov/about-federal-courts/probation-and-pretrial-services) · [U.S. Courts: Role of the Pretrial Services Officer](https://www.ksp.uscourts.gov/role-pretrial-services-officer)
- **Treatment organizations need both clinical and administrative roles.** SAMHSA's CCBHC model expects care coordination across behavioral health, physical health, social services, and other systems, plus person- and family-centered planning and peer/family support. This calls for organization-level programs, teams, referral coordination, staff administration, and privacy segmentation; it is not satisfied by putting a facility name on an individual's provider profile. [SAMHSA: Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinics](https://www.samhsa.gov/communities/certified-community-behavioral-health-clinics)

## Workforce coverage to plan for

| Workspace track | Roles to support | Main work surface | Data boundary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Primary and medical care | PCP/physician, nurse practitioner, physician assistant, RN/LPN, pharmacist, nutrition professional | Appointment follow-up, client-authorized coordination, referrals, care-team handoffs | WellnessCafe is not an EHR; do not add medication lists, diagnoses, or clinical notes without a separately reviewed clinical-record design |
| Behavioral-health treatment | Therapist, counselor, psychologist, psychiatrist, SUD clinician, behavioral-health care manager, psychiatric consultant | Connection and appointment coordination, client-chosen practices/resources, explicit follow-up | No automatic exposure of check-ins, reflections, or AI conversations; measurement-based care requires explicit product and privacy review |
| Case management and navigation | Social-work case manager, care coordinator, community health worker, housing/reentry/resource navigator | Client-defined goals, referral status, barriers, warm handoffs, follow-up ownership | Share only information needed for a specific client-authorized coordination task |
| Peer and recovery support | Certified peer specialist, peer recovery coach, family/youth peer, recovery mentor | Client-led support contact, community connection, optional practices and resource linkage | Preserve peer role boundaries; do not relabel lived-experience support as licensed clinical care |
| Community supervision | Probation/pretrial/parole officer and authorized supervision staff | Referral handoff and explicitly approved logistics, if later legally and operationally approved | Separate tenant, policy, and record surface. Never reuse clinical client panels or infer consent from a court/supervision relationship |
| Service organization | Treatment center/CCBHC, private group practice, community-health organization, peer organization | Program/location directory, staff membership, referral intake, schedule coordination, operational aggregates | Tenant membership is not client-data permission; each person and each information scope still needs an explicit server-checked basis |

Adjacent roles surfaced by the research—nurses, pharmacists, community health workers, behavioral-health care managers, psychiatric consultants, peer/family workers, intake and referral coordinators, and organization supervisors—should be considered in the taxonomy. They should not all receive a newly created account type immediately; access follows actual workflows, jurisdictional credential rules, and verified capabilities.

## Recommended architecture

1. **Separate identity, occupation, organization, and permission.** Keep the signed-in account identity distinct from a professional classification, verified credential, workspace kind, organization membership, and actionable permission. A title or organization field is not an authorization claim.
2. **Add organizations as first-class tenants.** A future organization model needs verified organization identity, locations/programs, staff memberships, invitations, membership state, and role assignment. Use granular roles such as organization owner, workspace administrator, clinical supervisor, referral coordinator, practitioner, and scheduling/intake staff. Every server operation checks current membership and least-privilege scope.
3. **Keep person-to-person connection and consent separate from tenant membership.** Being employed by the same treatment center, sharing a provider directory, or receiving a referral must not add a client to every staff member's caseload. A direct, current assignment plus client-controlled, purpose-specific sharing scopes is required for any private client data.
4. **Build dashboards from capabilities and work queues, not a hard-coded title.** Role presets determine default panels and actions; server grants decide which data requests can succeed. This supports hybrid staff and prevents a dashboard label from becoming a permission check.
5. **Keep records fit for this product.** First dashboards should coordinate appointments, referrals, consent, practice recommendations, and client-chosen updates already supported by current services. Do not add an EHR, court-case-management system, clinical notes, probation conditions, diagnosis, medication management, automated risk scoring, or cross-organization record exchange as a cosmetic dashboard expansion.
6. **Plan compliance before organization data.** Before onboarding clinics, treatment centers, or justice organizations, complete a formal HIPAA/42 CFR Part 2 and state/jurisdiction review, role and data-flow inventory, retention/export/deletion plan, business-associate/vendor review, audit design, incident response, and an assessment of how voluntary client consent works where a relationship can carry legal or institutional power. Do not market this as certified or compliant until qualified review establishes that.

## Evidence-to-product review: where the value belongs

The research favors a few complete service loops over a larger catalog of disconnected exercises. This section translates established workflows into product requirements; it does **not** claim that WellnessCafe has tested these workflows or that a digital feature will itself improve clinical outcomes.

| Evidence-informed practice | Product implication | Where it belongs in WellnessCafe | How to evaluate without overclaiming |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Appointment preparation and question prompts.** AHRQ provides prep cards and a Question Builder for visit reason, concerns, questions, and goals; it recommends inviting questions and measuring patient feedback. | Make the client the author; keep preparation short, editable, and private by default. Let them select individual items to share and optionally export a readable one-page summary. | A new client-side Visit Prep service linked to appointments; practitioner sees it only after an explicit share grant and can acknowledge it. | Pilot usability, completion, user-reported preparedness, whether the client felt heard, and unanswered-question follow-up. Do not claim improved health outcomes based on task completion alone. |
| **Shared decision-making.** AHRQ's SHARE steps ask the practitioner to invite participation, compare options and their benefits/harms, assess values, decide together, and plan evaluation. AHRQ's evidence summary reports decision aids can improve knowledge, risk estimates, involvement, and clarification of preferences. | Add a client-authored “what matters to me / questions / what I understood / what I want to revisit” handoff to support conversations. Do not have WellnessCafe rank clinical options. | Visit Prep now; a future licensed-clinician decision-support workflow only after clinical content governance and maintenance are in place. | Test comprehension and whether users can edit or reject summaries. Keep clinical decision aid efficacy claims tied to validated, current, topic-specific aids, not generated content. |
| **Care coordination and closed-loop referrals.** AHRQ describes consent-aware team communication, warm handoffs, and active follow-up; it warns that a referral sent is not proof of a successful transition. | Treat referral as a stateful shared task: named owner, next due date, limited information scope, client-selected contact method, status updates, barrier support, and explicit closure/alternate plan. | A new client-visible referral and handoff service for primary care and care coordination; dashboard queue shows only assigned, authorized tasks. | Measure referral acknowledgement, contact, appointment/connection confirmation, unresolved reason, and time to next action. Report denominators and missing status; never imply a referral was completed when status is unknown. |
| **Collaborative behavioral-health care.** CMS describes a defined team (treating practitioner, behavioral-health care manager, psychiatric consultant) plus registry-based follow-up and validated outcome measures. | Build role-specific team assignments and work queues only if the operational care model and qualified clinical governance exist. Do not copy just the visual layout of a registry. | Later organization/integrated-care track; current workspace can support appointment coordination and client-shared wellness preferences, not CoCM. | Only evaluate as the actual governed service model. Do not market the current product as collaborative care, measurement-based care, or reimbursable CoCM. |
| **Recovery-oriented peer support.** SAMHSA's competencies define peer support as person-centered, voluntary, relationship-focused, trauma-informed, and grounded in self-direction and multiple recovery pathways. | Keep practice invitations optional, personalize them to the client's stated goal, make decline/skip normal, and preserve peer support as distinct from licensed treatment. Do not infer recovery success from tool opens, streaks, or disclosure volume. |

## Embed value in the tools already offered

The existing tool catalog is a reasonable starting library, but opening an exercise should not be the whole service. Add an optional, client-controlled **before / during / after** structure that turns a practice into a meaningful next step without making it a clinical assessment:

1. **Before:** “What would help right now?” with a few plain-language choices (settle, focus, make a plan, reflect, or something else), an option to skip, and a clear explanation of what is private. A practitioner can send a specific tool only through the existing authorized relationship; the client decides whether to open it.
2. **During:** Deliver the modality-specific experience already being built—voice and motion for guided practices, panoramic imagery for scene-based meditation, short visual steps where useful. Keep a visible pause/stop/skip control and a non-audio path. Do not interrupt a guided exercise with surveys or require bodily attention.
3. **After:** Offer one small optional reflection: “Was this useful?”, “What would you like to do next?”, or “Would you like to share this update with [named practitioner]?” The content remains private unless selected. Share a small structured signal (tool completed/skipped, client-selected usefulness, or requested next step) rather than raw journaling by default.
4. **Follow through:** If the client asks for human help, create an explicit request with a selected recipient, allowed context, and acknowledgement status. Do not turn a generic tool completion into a risk flag or clinical inference.

This pattern can attach to breathing, PMR, grounding, urge surfing, meditation, body scan, journaling, and low-energy planning while preserving each tool's distinctive experience. It should not force every tool into the same script: imagery stays visual, journaling stays private-first, and body-focused practices always allow external focus or stopping. Begin with client-controlled usefulness and next-step requests; only add practitioner-visible completion summaries after capability, consent, revocation, retention, and audit behavior are specified and server-enforced.

### Build order and proof gate

1. **Make current tools useful end-to-end:** consistent privacy explanation, voluntary start/stop, accessible session state, optional usefulness feedback, and a clear “ask a person for support” path. Use existing tool modules; do not create duplicated surface-only tiles.
2. **Verify and pilot Visit Prep:** emulator-test two-account authorization, field-level projection, appointment cancellation/expiry, scope and connection revocation, acknowledgement, delete/export, then validate the language and flow with clients and licensed primary-care practitioners. The current source implementation is not a production release until this gate passes.
3. **Build the referral loop:** client choice, destination/contact, minimum necessary handoff fields, explicit consent snapshot, owner, due date, status transitions, warm introduction, retry/alternate option, closure, and immediate revocation behavior. Separate capabilities for client creation, practitioner task access, organization assignment, and status update.
4. **Organization workspace:** only after tenant, membership, assignments, role grants, audit, and removal/revocation checks exist. Start with referral/intake coordination, not broad clinical records or whole-org client lists.
5. **Role expansion:** stage primary care and care coordination after distinct server capability sets and verified review; add pharmacist and other roles only with matching workflows; keep probation/community supervision outside the clinical workspace.

For each release, define: the exact user job, client and practitioner steps, empty/error/revocation states, accessible mobile and low-literacy path, server authorization matrix, audit event, and a small pilot metric set. Product success should be judged by useful completed handoffs and client-reported usefulness—not visual polish, generated activity, number of screens, or “multi-million-dollar” branding. None of these research recommendations by itself establishes HIPAA, 42 CFR Part 2, clinical effectiveness, or reimbursement readiness.

### Consent revocation hardening — 2026-10-12

Turning off the practical-help sharing scope increments a per-practitioner consent version. Existing requests carry the version under which the client shared them. Practitioner reads and updates require an exact match, so turning the scope back on does not silently restore old requests. The client tracker identifies requests that need renewed consent and offers an explicit per-request reshare action only while the connection, scope, and global sharing service are active. Connection removal and global sharing pause continue to block practitioner access. This server and UI behavior is implemented in source but is not deployed or emulator/two-account verified.

The production build passes; these changes have not been deployed or verified with a signed-in provider account.

Both **Privacy & your data** and **Your practitioner sharing** now download a `.txt` report with plain-language sections and readable fields instead of raw JSON. Internal record/account/assignment identifiers and practitioner-sharing metadata are omitted. The export remains owner-scoped and includes the same available account records as before; this presentation change does not widen sharing or export access. The file states that it is a private copy and is not sent to a practitioner.

This is a value and navigation improvement for currently enabled roles, not completion of the larger dashboard program. The current case-management queue still lacks a maintained referral destination, explicit named task owner, due date, contact-attempt history, and alternate referral/closure workflow. Treatment organizations still require tenant/membership authorization; probation/pretrial/parole remain a separate product and privacy review. These tracks must not be represented as enabled dashboards until their distinct server capabilities and lifecycle tests exist.
